Color Contrast Analyzer

Check whether a foreground and background pair meets WCAG contrast requirements, and get harmonious alternatives when it does not. Contrast is the accessibility issue most likely to be flagged in an audit.

WCAG contrast is a ratio between the relative luminance of two colours, ranging from 1:1 to 21:1. The thresholds that matter are 4.5:1 for normal body text at AA, 3:1 for large text (18pt, or 14pt bold) and for interface components, and 7:1 for AAA. Those numbers are also what accessibility audits and an increasing number of procurement checklists test against.

Luminance is not brightness as you perceive it, which is why a yellow that looks vivid can fail against white while a mid-grey passes. The ratio is computed here for the pair you enter, with the pass and fail marked per threshold, so a palette decision can be made before the design is built rather than after an audit.

list_alt How to use Color Contrast Analyzer

  1. Enter your foreground and background colours as hex or rgb().
  2. Read the contrast ratio and whether it passes AA and AAA for normal and large text.
  3. If it fails, pick a suggested harmony colour and apply it to either layer.
  4. Copy the colour you settle on.

lightbulb Tips & gotchas

  • The thresholds are 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text at AA; AAA asks for 7:1 and 4.5:1. Large means roughly 18.66px bold or 24px regular.
  • Grey placeholder text and light-grey borders are where contrast failures cluster. Check those, not just body copy.
  • Contrast alone is not accessibility — never use colour as the only way to convey meaning.

help_outline Frequently asked questions

What contrast ratio do I actually need?

Aim for 4.5:1 on body text as a baseline. That is the AA requirement and what most audits and legal standards reference.

Do icons and form borders need to pass?

Yes. WCAG asks for 3:1 on meaningful non-text elements such as icons, focus rings and input borders.

Does contrast change with font size?

The requirement does. Large text is allowed a lower ratio because thicker strokes are easier to read at the same contrast.
